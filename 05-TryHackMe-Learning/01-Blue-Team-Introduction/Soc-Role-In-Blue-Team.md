## Security Hierarchy and the Blue Team
### Room Overview
This room explored how cybersecurity responsibilities are organised within a company, where a Security Operations Centre (SOC) fits into that structure, and how different defensive security roles work together.
### Task 1: Introduction
The room builds on the Junior Security Analyst Intro room by looking beyond the day-to-day work of an L1 analyst. It introduces the wider company security structure, the teams that support security operations, and possible career paths.
### Task 2: Security Hierarchy
### Company Security Structure
A company may organise security responsibilities across four broad levels:
1. Executives (CEO, CFO or company owner) – Focus on the organisation's overall business objectives and priorities.
2. Security Leadership (CTO, CIO or CISO) – Leads the organisation's technology or security programme.
3. Security Managers (SOC Manager or Team Lead) – Manage a security team or department and coordinate its work.
4. Technical Specialists – Carry out practical work such as alert analysis, security engineering and penetration testing.
The exact structure varies between organisations. In smaller companies, several responsibilities may sit within one IT or information security team.
### Main Security Departments
- Red Team: Offensive security specialists, such as penetration testers and ethical hackers, who look for security weaknesses.
- GRC Team: Specialists who manage policies, risk and compliance with requirements such as PCI DSS.
- Blue Team: Defensive security specialists, including SOC analysts, security engineers and incident responders.
### Common SOC Roles
- L1 Analyst: Reviews and triages alerts, performs initial investigation and escalates complex cases to L2.
- L2 Analyst: Conducts more advanced investigations and handles cases escalated by L1.
- SOC Engineer: Configures and maintains security tools, such as SIEM and EDR platforms.
- SOC Manager: Manages the SOC team and oversees its operations.
### Task 3: Meet the Blue Team
### Security Operations Centre (SOC)
The SOC is a central part of an organisation's defensive security operation. It monitors security activity, investigates alerts and coordinates responses to potential threats.

The room highlighted common SOC activities:
- Monitoring and collecting logs.
- Investigating security alerts.
- Creating and maintaining detection rules.
- Working with IT teams.
- Escalating suspicious activity.
- Producing summary reports.
A SOC may operate continuously, including 24/7 coverage, depending on the organisation.
### Cyber Incident Response Team (CIRT)
A Cyber Incident Response Team deals with significant cyber incidents that require a coordinated specialist response. It may support the SOC when an incident is serious or complex.

Examples introduced in the room included:
- **JPCERT**: Japan's national computer emergency response team.
- **Mandiant**: A private organisation that provides incident response and other cybersecurity services.
- **AWS CIRT**: Amazon's incident response team for security incidents involving AWS customers.
### Specialised Defensive Roles
- **Digital Forensics Analyst**: Examines digital evidence, such as disk and memory artefacts, to understand what happened.
- **Threat Intelligence Analyst**: Gathers and analyses information about threat actors, campaigns and emerging threats.
- **Application Security Engineer**: Helps maintain a secure software development lifecycle.
- **AI Researcher**: Studies AI-related threats and defensive applications.
### Task 4: Advancing a SOC Career
### Internal SOC vs MSSP
An **Internal SOC** is a security operations team employed by an organisation to protect that organisation's own systems and data.

A **Managed Security Service Provider (MSSP)** is an external provider that delivers agreed security services to other organisations. An MSSP may monitor several customers.
|Area	|Internal SOC |	MSSP
|---|---|---|
Main focus	|One organisation|	Multiple customer organisations
Environment	|Develops deeper familiarity with its employer's environment	|Works across different customer environments
Communication	|Internal IT, security and business teams|	Customer contacts and the provider's own teams
Priorities|	The employer's security priorities|	Customer priorities and agreed service levels
Learning exposure	|In-depth knowledge of one environment|	Exposure to different environments and tools


Neither setting is automatically better for a beginner. The quality of training, supervision, tools and opportunities to investigate incidents matters.
### Career Development
The room presents SOC L1 as a possible starting point. With experience and continued learning, an analyst may progress towards L2 work or explore other paths, such as security engineering, incident response, threat intelligence or other specialised defensive roles.

Career progression is not necessarily a single fixed ladder. It depends on practical experience, interests, skills and available opportunities.
### Task 5: Final Challenge
The final challenge involved matching security scenarios to the specialist role best placed to handle them.

The main lesson was that incidents and tasks should be directed to people with the appropriate expertise. Examples include:
- **Routine security alert**: SOC analyst.
- **Major or active cyber incident**: CIRT or incident response team.
- **Compliance and audit requirements**: GRC specialist.
- **Vulnerability testing**: Penetration tester.
- **Threat actor tactics and campaigns**: Threat intelligence analyst.
The challenge reinforced the importance of understanding team responsibilities and knowing when to escalate rather than trying to handle every issue alone.
### Key Learning Outcomes
After completing this room, I can:
- Explain how security responsibilities may be arranged from executive leadership to technical teams.
- Distinguish between Red, Blue and GRC teams.
- Describe the responsibilities of L1 analysts, L2 analysts, SOC engineers and SOC managers.
- Explain how a SOC and a CIRT contribute to defensive security.
- Identify the purpose of specialised roles such as digital forensics, threat intelligence and application security.
- Explain the difference between an Internal SOC and an MSSP.
- Recognise why incidents and tasks should be escalated to the appropriate specialists.
### Personal Reflection
This room helped me understand that a SOC analyst is part of a wider security function rather than working in isolation. An effective analyst needs to recognise the limits of their role, document findings clearly and escalate work to the right team when specialist support is needed.

The comparison between an Internal SOC and an MSSP also showed me that security operations can be organised in different ways. Both models require sound investigation, communication, consistency and an understanding of the environment being protected.

My main takeaway is that knowing who is responsible for what is just as important as knowing how to identify a security alert.
